# Vision & audience

## The gap in the market

The god game genre has been essentially dormant since *Black & White 2* (2005) and the *Godus* flameout (2013). Meanwhile, three adjacent audiences have exploded and nobody serves all of them at once:

| Comparable | What it proves | Where it stops short |
|---|---|---|
| **WorldBox** | Millions of players want a god-sandbox that simulates emergent civs. Its community constantly begs for more depth and lore. | 2D pixel art, shallow history, no real religion/faith systems. |
| **Dwarf Fortress (Legends mode)** | Generated history with named figures, myths, and wars is *deeply* compelling. | Notoriously hostile presentation; history is generated, not authored by the player. |
| **Black & White** | Being *perceived* by your worshippers (fear vs. love, world tone shifting) is a magical mechanic. | 2001 tech, village-scale, abandoned genre. |
| **Manor Lords** | A tiny team can hit gorgeous, grounded, "real place" visuals that market themselves. | One village, no god layer, no deep time. |
| **Azgaar's Fantasy Map Generator / Inkarnate / Wonderdraft** | A huge D&D/worldbuilder crowd will spend hours making *maps alone*, with no game attached. | It's a drawing, not a living world. |
| **The Universim / Fata Deum** | People still buy god games on sight. | Both landed "wide but shallow" — the cautionary tale for us. |
| **RimWorld / Crusader Kings** | "Story generator" is a durable, top-selling genre framing. | Colony/character scale, not god scale. |

**SLATE's slot:** *WorldBox's sandbox × Dwarf Fortress's chronicle × Black & White's being-worshipped fantasy × Manor Lords' presentation.* Each pairing exists; the intersection doesn't.

## The killer feature for the stated audience

**The world is a campaign setting forge.** After a long play, you export:

- Maps at every zoom level (illustrated atlas style or realistic).
- A gazetteer of regions, cities, cultures, and religions — including *what actually happened* vs. *what each culture believes happened* (instant unreliable-narrator lore).
- Timelines, dynasty trees, religion schism diagrams.
- Notable figures with deeds, epithets, and relationships ("Osric the Riverburner, who defied the Storm God and drowned for it").

A D&D DM plays SLATE for twenty relaxing hours and walks away with a homebrew setting with 4,000 years of coherent canon. That's not a feature, that's a *reason to buy* — and it's cheap relative to its value, because the chronicle data has to exist for the sim anyway.

## Audience personas

1. **The Worldbuilder (primary).** DMs, fantasy writers, map nerds, r/worldbuilding. Wants authorship, depth, export, internal consistency. Plays 100+ hours solo. Evangelizes in niche communities.
2. **The Terrarium Gazer.** WorldBox/ant-farm player. Wants to poke the world and watch it react. Session length 30–90 min. Wants spectacle and emergence, tolerates less depth.
3. **The Co-op Group.** 2–4 friends sharing a world as a pantheon. Wants low-pressure collaborative play with drama that comes from the world, not from PvP.
4. **The Streamer.** Wants legible spectacle, chat participation hooks, and stories that generate clips. A god game where chat members are named villagers is a self-marketing machine.

Design tiebreaker rule: when two features conflict, favor the Worldbuilder — they're the identity of the game and the deepest well of goodwill. The Gazer is the top of the funnel; the Streamer is the marketing department.

## Positioning & business notes (light, for later)

- Premium indie ($25–35 tier), no F2P mechanics ever — poison for this audience.
- Expansion-friendly by design: "Modern Age" (the 2026-dragon-in-NYC fantasy), pantheon multiplayer, mythology packs.
- Mod support is a pillar, not an afterthought: the sim is data-driven and the event log is open. This audience *will* build content for us (cultures, creatures, chroniclers) if we let them.
- "Chill" is a marketing stance, not just a vibe: no fail state, pausable always, ambient score, Bob-Ross-of-god-games energy.
